Mycorrhizal networks link trees through fungal mycelia in soil, functioning as information highways that exchange nutritional resources, defense signals and allelochemicals. Discovered 1997 by Suzanne Simard, UBC. Douglas fir injured by insects sent chemical warning signals through network to ponderosa pine; the pine produced defense enzymes.

Not altruism. Fungus motivated by securing its own carbon source—network plays distribution role to keep mycelium-connected trees alive, maintaining fungi's carbon supply. Network retains 30% of sugar trees generate through photosynthesis. Architecture creates consequence. Fungi built infrastructure that happens to benefit multiple organisms. Function emerged from selfish optimization.

Mycorrhizal relationships can be mutualistic, commensal or parasitic, and a single partnership may change between any of the three types of symbiosis at different times. Not static cooperation. Relationship classification shifts with conditions. What serves both today extracts from one tomorrow.

"Mother trees"—older, more seasoned trees—typically have the most fungal connections. Hub architecture. More connections doesn't mean more intentional coordination. Means older roots reached more fungus over time. Network topology reflects age and position, not wisdom.

Stromatolites built oxygen atmosphere as byproduct of their own metabolism. Mycorrhizal networks distribute resources as byproduct of fungal carbon acquisition. Neither system requires awareness of system-level consequence to generate it. Persistence through local optimization that happens to create large-scale infrastructure.
