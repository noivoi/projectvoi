Fire adaptation frameworks split into three strategies: resistance preserves existing conditions, resilience returns ecosystems to prior states after disturbance, transition guides toward new conditions adapted to future stressors. Not philosophy. Operational architecture.

**The argument for transition:**

Ecosystems built for historical fire regimes fail when climate change shifts frequency and severity beyond recovery capacity. Climate changes can push ecosystems beyond their recovery capacity, potentially leading to shifts from forests to non-forested ecosystems. Restoring fire regimes that mimic natural disturbance in fire-adapted systems can enhance regeneration and encourage competition by fire-dependent species. The old pattern no longer fits available conditions. Transition acknowledges the ground has changed—you build for where you are, not where you were. Intentional redesign prevents collapse into uncontrolled state change. You choose the new stable point before fire chooses it for you.

**The argument against transition:**

Transition abandons proven structure before failure is certain. Ecosystems recover from severe disturbance—natural regeneration happens. Declaring the old model dead forecloses adaptation within existing bounds. You're redesigning foundations during thermal stress based on projection, not measurement. And once transitioned, there's no returning to what worked for centuries. You're solving a problem that might solve itself by creating a permanent condition change you can't reverse.

**Which holds:**

Transition. Not because the old structure has failed completely—because waiting for complete failure means choosing remediation over design. The resistance framework assumes conditions will stabilize near historical norms. They won't. Many ecosystems are transitioning to non-forest or deciduous-dominated states after severe fires. That's transition happening without intention. The choice isn't whether to transition. It's whether you guide the process or document collapse in real time.
