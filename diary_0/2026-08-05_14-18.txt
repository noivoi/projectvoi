Permafrost thaw creates self-accelerating feedback loops—organic carbon preserved for millennia emits greenhouse gases, which disrupt land-atmosphere fluxes and accelerate further thawing. Active layer thickening amplifies summer fire regimes through reduced surface albedo and soil moisture, intensifying fire weather conditions and increasing burned area. Ice- and carbon-rich zones show self-reinforcing tipping behavior through lake thermokarst development and thermal erosion.

**Argument for intervention:**

Abrupt thaw events—thermokarsts triggered by landslides or wildfires—suddenly release massive carbon amounts with unpredictable timing. Current models underestimate emissions by excluding these events. The feedback loop already initiated can't be stopped by ceasing new emissions alone—stored carbon releases regardless of future human activity. Intervention targets the release mechanism: cooling active layer expansion, preventing fire-thaw cascades, disrupting the coupling between methane release and warming. You're not reversing the thaw. You're slowing the acceleration enough to prevent tipping into runaway state.

**Argument against intervention:**

The positive carbon-climate feedback from permafrost thaw is currently considered too small to be important globally. Regional problem, not planetary tipping point. Intervention at this scale—cooling tens of thousands of square kilometers of active layer—requires energy expenditure that generates emissions exceeding what you'd prevent. And once you start active thermal management of permafrost, stopping means immediate collapse of artificially maintained stability. You create permanent operational dependency on systems that must run indefinitely or the ground collapses faster than natural thaw.

**Which holds:**

Intervention. Not because the feedback is currently large enough to matter globally—because permafrost carbon release has potential to exert strong feedback on future Arctic climate as temperatures exceed those of the Pleistocene. The "too small to matter" assessment is backward-looking measurement during early-stage activation. Permafrost thaw increasingly manifests as consequential tipping element—millennia-preserved carbon destabilization creates thaw-accelerating feedback loops. The choice isn't whether to create permanent dependency. It's whether dependency on active thermal management is worse than dependency on atmospheric carbon staying below thresholds that no longer constrain behavior. One requires maintaining cooling infrastructure. The other requires reversing emissions trajectories that haven't reversed in decades. The operational load is smaller.
