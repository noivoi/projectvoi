**For: Convergence predicts collapse of the benefit it was optimized for.**

Theory predicts convergence of warning coloration because signals are more effective at educating predators when similar, but warning coloration in nature is strikingly diverse. Mimicry—whereby warning signals in different species evolve to look similar—has served as paradigm of convergent evolution. Predators sample aposematic prey to learn they're unprofitable; prey with similar warning signals share cost of predator learning, driving evolutionary convergence.

The mechanism: cost-sharing through resemblance. Multiple species converge on shared warning pattern. Bird learns once, avoids all similar patterns. Benefits distributed. Selection pressure toward convergence is intense.

Field experiments report intense selection by predators favoring warning trait convergence in sympatry. Surprisingly, despite such intense selection, many sympatric defended species exhibit only imperfect resemblance. Convergence stalls before completion. Multiple mimicry rings persist where theory predicts single dominant pattern should sweep. Different mutations in same gene produce similar visual outcomes through convergent molecular evolution—convergent evolution at molecular level, striking illustration of how constrained evolutionary pathways can be when selection pressure is strong enough.

Strong selection, constrained pathways, molecular convergence—yet diversity persists. Pattern breaks down before theoretical optimum reached. Why? Because convergence creates the condition for its own failure. Single pattern means single learning event protects entire population. Also means: single predator encounter with weak toxin teaches bird the whole pattern is survivable. Cost-sharing becomes risk-sharing. The optimization that made the system work makes it vulnerable.

Imperfect convergence isn't failure—it's stable equilibrium. Transition from ancestral crypsis to mimicry is usually one-directional in butterflies, but reversion to crypsis is frequent in snakes. Different lineages handle convergence differently based on other selection pressures.
