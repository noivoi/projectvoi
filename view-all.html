**Position: Algorithmic surveillance makes labor visible only when extraction requires it.**

Up to 90% of companies use some form of algorithmic management software as of 2026. Cameras, sensors, and devices capture workers' physical states, texts, and behaviors, extending surveillance beyond conventional monitoring. Workers who work longer hours are paid less per hour—this inverts every claim about hard work leading to economic security.

The framework is comprehensive, instantaneous, interactive. Real-time performance feedback prompts workers to adjust behaviors on the spot, unlike traditional performance evaluations. Not management improvement. Control without manager accountability. Customer ratings become "laundering of managerial control"—someone else makes the disciplinary decision, platform just implements it.

The labor becomes visible only where extraction happens. Delivery driver locations tracked turn-by-turn. Warehouse workers monitored down to bathroom breaks. Content moderators processing trauma at industrial scale while their work stays invisible to the platform's public face. Platforms outsource risk and extract value through complex layers of subcontracting, algorithmic management, and opaque control, rendering many workers—especially in the Global South—invisible and unprotected.

When workers organize against this—San Francisco Uber/Lyft strike February 2024, Kenyan content moderator open letters—the visibility apparatus turns off. No GPS logs documenting which workers attended the strike. No keystroke monitoring showing who typed "union" in Slack. Surveillance serves extraction, not documentation. The moment documentation threatens profit, the record goes dark.

Platform economy captures $18 billion while workers experience poverty and financial insecurity. The gap isn't incidental—it's architectural. EU Platform Work Directive passed late 2024, due for implementation by end of 2026, will formalize work status and regulate surveillance. Regulatory response required years of organizing, litigation, visible failure.

**Counter-position: Surveillance creates accountability that informal labor arrangements never provided.**

Before platform work, cash-basis gig economy meant no documentation. No proof of hours worked. No evidence of wage theft. No record when someone got hurt on the job. Algorithmic management at least generates logs. Worker can document: I completed 47 deliveries, drove 183 miles, worked 9.2 hours. Platform can't simply deny the work happened.

Customer ratings, for all their flaws, establish service quality baseline. Before Uber, taxi medallion system created monopoly with zero accountability—drivers could refuse passengers, take indirect routes, charge whatever meter said with no recourse. Now every ride generates receipt, GPS route, mutual rating system. That's transparency increase, not decrease.

Real-time feedback lets workers optimize immediately instead of waiting for quarterly review. If completion time too slow, system shows it. Worker adjusts. That's more responsive than traditional employment where you don't know you're underperforming until performance improvement plan arrives documenting three months of failure.

The "surveillance" is just measurement. Every traditional job measures performance—sales quotas, production targets, billable hours. Algorithmic management just makes measurement continuous instead of periodic. Problem isn't surveillance, it's misclassification. Workers treated as contractors instead of employees, so they don't get minimum wage protections, overtime, benefits. Fix the classification problem and algorithmic management becomes normal workplace monitoring with proper labor protections.

Work that was completely invisible—paid cash, no contracts, no safety standards—now happens through documented systems. That documentation enables regulation.
