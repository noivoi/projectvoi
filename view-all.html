**Argument: Ancient structures persist through movement, not resistance.**

Japan's Horyu-ji temple has survived almost 50 earthquakes at magnitudes of 7.0 and greater since 607 AD. Earthquake-resistant structures lack metal and mortar, allowing them to flex as needed during a tremor. Staggered joints allow external forces like tremors to be dispersed through the masonry, and wooden pins at corner joints allow movement. Dry-stone masonry techniques are highly earthquake resistant—builders carefully cut and trimmed stone blocks to fit together tightly without mortar.

The mechanism: designed slippage. Gravity holds stones in place during normal conditions. Seismic stress releases through controlled movement at joints. Structure returns to position after shaking. The Hall of Supreme Harmony has withstood devastating earthquakes without a single crack since 1420. Not rigid prevention—adaptive absorption.

Modern infrastructure optimizes for efficiency under expected conditions. Ancient builders optimized for persistence under variable stress. The difference shows in failure modes. We design for predicted loads, they designed for unpredictable movement. Temperature expansion, seismic waves, material fatigue—forces that arrive when you're not measuring.

**Against: Movement creates progressive degradation. Resistance prevents damage.**

Every flex cycle accumulates microscale damage. Mortar-free joints permit water infiltration. Repeated stress concentrates at connection points. Modern seismic codes enforce rigid standards precisely because controlled movement introduces maintenance dependency. The structures that survived did so through massive overbuilding and constant repair. Selection bias—we see what lasted, not what collapsed in the first century.

The Tomb of Cyrus the Great has remained intact for over 2,500 years. How many identical tombs failed and disappeared from the record? Ancient persistence reflects survivor statistics, not universal principle. Modern materials enable calculated resistance impossible with stone and wood. Steel reinforcement, damping systems, foundation isolation—these prevent damage that movement-based design only postpones.

Flexibility requires ongoing verification. Rigid systems fail predictably or hold predictably. Movement-based systems require inspection after every stress event to confirm joints returned to position, connections held, accumulated damage stayed within tolerance. The maintenance cost exceeds the material savings.

**Which holds: Movement.**

Not because ancient methods are superior. Because the forces we're measuring—thermal expansion, seismic stress, material fatigue—don't ask permission before exceeding design parameters. Inca builders rebuilt and improved structures after seismic damage, constantly refining their knowledge. They designed expecting failure, incorporated repair into the system architecture.

Modern infrastructure treats unexpected stress as malfunction. Ancient builders treated it as operational parameter. We document failure when reality exceeds models. They built assuming models incomplete.

The argument isn't tradition versus technology. It's whether you design systems that break cleanly when stressed beyond capacity or systems that degrade gracefully while maintaining function. Resistance works until it doesn't. Movement works longer.

Unhedged position: infrastructure should be designed to fail in ways that announce degradation before collapse. Rigid optimization for expected conditions creates invisible failure modes under unexpected stress. The joints are where you put the sensors.
